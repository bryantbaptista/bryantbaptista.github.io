## Asking Questions as a Software Engineer

As a current university student, I find myself getting stuck on problems all the time. After reading Eric Raymond's [*How to Ask Questions The Smart Way*](http://www.catb.org/esr/faqs/smart-questions.html), I learned the importance of asking a proper, or "smart," question when you are stuck on a problem. Of course, there is no such thing as a "bad" question, but there are ways to ask questions more effectively.

According to Raymond, it is always a good idea to research the problem on your own before asking others for help. If you still cannot find a solution, you should provide as much relevant information and context as possible so that others can better understand the problem and give you a useful answer. Below are examples of how to properly ask a "smart" question compared to a "not so smart" question.

## Smart vs Not So Smart Questions on Stack Overflow

An example of a "smart" question that I found on Stack Overflow was this developer asking [why removing an unused class member slowed down their C++ program](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/79996455/removing-an-unused-class-member-slows-down-program).

One reason why I would consider this a "smart" question based on Raymond's precepts is the amount of context provided. The developer explained the unexpected behavior they were confused about, their exact hardware specifications, sample code with benchmark results, and their own hypothesis that the issue might be related to memory layout and caching. This shows that the developer attempted to find a reason behind the problem rather than simply asking for help right away.

Additionally, the developer focused on asking for clarification about the specific case they described rather than asking a broad question. Because of this, another person was able to provide a detailed answer with technical explanations and a potential fix without needing to ask for more information.

An example of a "not so smart" question that I found on Stack Overflow was [this question regarding extracting duplicate characters from a string](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/76677968/extract-duplicate-characters-from-a-string).

The first thing that stood out was the developer admitting that they did not make any attempt to solve the problem. This goes against Raymond's precept of making a serious attempt or researching the problem before asking for help. Additionally, the developer broadly asks for several ways to solve the assignment instead of pointing to exactly where they got stuck or what they needed clarification on. Due to the nature of the question, the responses were mainly solutions to the assignment rather than meaningful explanations of how to approach the problem.

## Insights on Smart Questions

Analyzing questions on Stack Overflow and comparing "smart" with "not so smart" questions really opened my eyes to the importance of Raymond's precepts. Being able to formulate "smart" questions not only benefits your communication skills, but also allows other people to effectively help you solve your problem. 

Ensuring that your questions are well formulated before posting prevents other people's time from being wasted and helps keep forums from becoming cluttered, overall improving the experience for everyone. I plan to take what I learned from this experience and apply it whenever I need assistance with a problem, both in and out of school.
